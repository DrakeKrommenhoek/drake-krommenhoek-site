## 1. Ordinary Time Traveler

- **Source:** "Drake Krommenhoek - Reflection: Ordinary Time Traveler" — `1gF89SCIPIKS694FB_YXk1IAN7XuPtrH9xO-QZJCO7pU` — modified 2024-10-22
- **Genre:** Personal essay (~1,180 words)
- **Recommendation:** **PUBLISH AFTER EDIT** — the flagship piece
- **Strongest theme:** Future Drake / living for a self who doesn't exist yet

**Why it's interesting.** This is the best writing he has ever done and it is also the origin document for the theme he is developing right now. He invents a character — "FD," an optimized future self — and treats him with real affection, then quietly admits the arrangement is a trade he isn't sure about: "*at the core, I care more about my future self than my present self.*" The essay opens by arguing for presence and ends by arguing for the chase, and never notices it has contradicted itself. **Do not fix that contradiction.** It is the whole reason a reader will finish it, and it's the exact tension the new writing section is supposed to explore.

**Editing needed.** Two passes only. (1) Cut the first sentence — "*Everyone knows we are the happiest when we're living in the present*" — a sweeping generalization he himself flags as his bad habit in the WRIT 100 reflection. Start on the second sentence, with the teacher and the poem. (2) The final paragraph repeats the "teeter-totter" summary that paragraph six already made; trim to the last three sentences. Everything else stands. Do not smooth the em-dashes into commas.

**Privacy.** His grandmother appears warmly; his family is described as loving. Low risk, but it's the doc that establishes the family-mentions precedent — get one blanket OK.

**Teaser (2–3 sentences, verbatim).**
> "The funny thing is, every kid wants to be an adult, and every adult wishes they could go back to being a kid. No one seems to want to be themselves in the moment. Maybe that's why we cling to our memories—because they give us an escape, a way to hold onto something that feels real."

**Strongest extractable passage (~490 words, verbatim).**

> I'm big into having a routine.  It may not be a rigid schedule, but just a general understanding of what I want to get done in order for Future Drake to be a happy man.  That doesn't mean I'm not happy currently; frankly, I couldn't be much happier where I'm at.  However, this does mean always doing something daily that gets me a little closer to Future Drake, or let's call him FD.  You see, FD is not a real person but an idea of what an optimized Present Drake would look like.  A celebrity who I think frames this the best is Matthew McConaughey.  When asked who his hero is, he would say, "Me in 10 years."  Then, ten years would pass, and he'd be asked, "So, are you your hero?" and he'd reply, "Not even close my hero's 10 years away."  It was never about becoming your own hero but always chasing the person you want to become.  My hero is FD.  It's hard to not think about the future, especially when you're not where you want to be in the present.  I always prophesize and think about the future, but I find comfort in my empty promises to FD.  If my present reality is not where past Drake hoped it would be, at least FD has it figured out.  No matter what curveballs I'm thrown, I know it all worked out, and it's going to be okay.  So, at the core, I care more about my future self than my present self, but the knowledge that my future self got through today gives me some security that I made it to tomorrow, and what better way to pay back to my future me than make today a great day.
>
> My greatest fear at all times was naps; I hated them with a passion. I'd even make up these little games with my babysitters just to subtract minutes from my naps. To me, avoiding naps was like a mission. And then there was my grandma—hugging her as hard as I could was non-negotiable. Now, I've flipped on the naps. They're planned into my day; I honestly don't know how I'd function without them. But when it comes to my grandma, I still give her those same hugs. It's funny how things change, and yet, some parts of who you are stay the same. As kids, we're fed all these "truths," and we just accept them because, well, that's what we're taught. It's not until later that we start questioning them and figuring out what really sticks.  I think we accept the beliefs and values that we are told should be important, and these don't become "core" values until we know why we believe them.

**Suggested titles (his register):** "Future Drake" · "My Hero Is Ten Years Away" · keep "Ordinary Time Traveler"

Whether it is due to underfunded government regulatory bodies or officials willing to look the other way for a cut of the profits, this approach allows companies to exploit cheap labor while evading accountability (Gunawan et al. 443).  The perfect crime; far enough from the producer that they do not have to see the conditions the workers are in, but getting their products for a fraction of the price than if made elsewhere.  This is not a broken system; it is a deliberately constructed one designed to reduce overhead and keep pace with consumer demand for low-cost, fast-turnaround fashion. The result is an extreme power imbalance, with producers holding all the control and garment workers left without any leverage, trapped in a cycle where human dignity is sacrificed for profitability.
>
> This is just the surface of how production really operates.  When the numbers look good on paper, demand rises, supply tightens, and profits soar.  The only solution?  More.  More clothes, more labor, more strain on everything except the wallet of the person pulling the strings.  Where to next when profits begin to plateau?  There must be a way to encourage customers to continue to buy even when they do not *need* anything anymore.
>
> [...]
>
> The fast fashion industry's ruthless efficiency is not innovation, but rather a form of legal erosion. As producers continue to choose to maximize profit despite available alternatives, they maintain a system that not only depletes natural resources and human labor but also erodes the necessary conditions for a sustainable future.  The longer these practices go unchallenged from within, the more normalized exploitation becomes when companies look to fast fashion as a model of economic success.  The cost of inaction, then, is not just environmental collapse or social injustice; it is the redefinition of what global commerce deems acceptable.  So what happens when exploitation becomes the blueprint, not the exception or simply a scary story of the past?  The monster was never hiding in your closet. It was building it.

**Suggested title:** keep it. It's already the best title he's written.

> What is art? The question that never seemed to leave our class from beginning to end, and we never came up with a definite answer for.  Is there a definite answer? By erasing a piece of Willem de Kooning's work, Rauschenberg created not just a blank page but an exploration of what makes something art at all.  I invite you to consider the story behind the work and the artist's intentions, shifting our focus from the result to the journey of its creation.
>
> Robert Rauschenberg's *Erased de Kooning* is more than a blank sheet of paper.  To create it, Rauschenberg approached Willem de Kooning, a highly respected artist, and asked for one of his drawings with the specific intention of erasing it.  Can you imagine being a highly esteemed artist and being approached by Rauschenberg, and he asks you for one of the paintings you're working on so he can erase it?  Holding a bottle of Jack Daniels and hoping your not home, and that will become his piece.  Blank canvas titled "de Kooning wasn't home" and that would be it. He's still hoping you say no so then he can create his piece "de Kooning said No" another blank canvas with a story.  But no, you take your work-in-progress painting off the easel, made with materials from oil paint to chalk for Rauschenberg to erase. >

The journey being more important and emphasized than the finished product. Rauschenberg's process was not easy. It highlighted the physical and emotional challenges that come with the personal process and what makes art so special. The project was not simply about erasing; it was about the idea of whether an act of destruction could also be one of creation. This difficulty became part of the artwork's meaning, illustrating how the process can hold more value than what we see with our eyes.
