### From the Summer 2026 Portable Record (2026-08-13) — his sharpest writing

**The three converged principles** — `[PUBLIC SAFE]`. He explicitly calls this "the most useful thing in the record," noting that "Modeling, diligence, AI enablement, and portfolio support arrived at the same three rules without being designed to."

> **"1. Separate construction from audit.** Building and checking are different mindsets requiring different caution levels. Auditing while still building produces false confidence."

> **"2. One change at a time, verified, before the next.** Batching edits hides interaction effects. Two errors can mask or compound each other, and fixing them in the wrong order makes a correct fix look like an overcorrection. Chaining unconfirmed assumptions is how hours of work get discarded. Confirming a single unit (one cell, one slide, one prompt, one candidate) before proceeding feels slower and is dramatically faster."

> **"3. Never smooth over a gap.** Do not plug a balance sheet discrepancy to make a check read OK. Do not let a proxy metric reach a committee without its caveat attached, because a number without its caveat becomes false precision. Do not let a placeholder like '[stat here]' survive into a final draft, because placeholders read as complete; write the actual sentence, badly if necessary, to expose whether the point exists. Flag weak evidence as weak evidence."

**Method notes** — `[PUBLIC SAFE]` (these are general craft, not firm methodology):
> "**Kill use cases whose premise fails.** Several attractive use cases depended on public-market data in a portfolio of exclusively private companies. They were cut rather than adapted. Checking the data premise before designing the workflow would have saved a week."

> "**Scope claims to the verified capability boundary.** The strongest single edit made to any use case narrowed an analytical assistant from 'supports analysis' to 'structures data and prepares for audit.' Narrower claims survive scrutiny, and the narrow version was the version that actually worked."

> "**Refuse to let one artifact do two jobs.** ... The training layer kept trying to absorb the reference material; separating them made both better."

> "**Workflow archaeology before design.** Map how the work actually gets done, not the org chart version. ... Nothing gets designed for a workflow that was not observed."

> "**Instructional design for a skeptical senior audience.** Cut all persuasion content, lead with a concrete task, make every claim demonstrable in the room."

> "**Anonymized real data is worse than well-constructed fake data,** because anonymization fails quietly."

**On delegation and AI limits** — `[PUBLIC SAFE]`, and the single most original idea in the corpus:
> "The axis is not easy/hard, it is whether a human can verify the output cheaply. Extraction, restructuring, and comparison are cheap to verify. Recommendations, credibility assessments, and forecast assumptions are not." ... "Survives model improvement: better models move the line, they do not erase it."

> "Near-universal AI deployment coexists with near-absent measured financial impact, so access is not the constraint. The constraint is shared method applied to real workflows with evidence collected."

> "**Evidence as a first-class deliverable** — A written record of what was deployed, who used it, what changed, and what failed. Design it at the start, because most of its content is unrecoverable afterward. Distinguishes a completed initiative from an announced one."

**What he'd do differently** — `[PUBLIC SAFE]`:
> "Check the data premise before designing any workflow. Design the evidence artifact in week one rather than week eight. Start the compliance conversation before there is anything to approve, so the gate is open when the work arrives at it. Separate the 'is this useful' question from the 'does this work' question, because conflating them produces material that argues when it should demonstrate. Lock the spec before building: the most expensive failures were builds started with one open question still live."

**Open questions he carried forward** — `[PUBLIC SAFE]`, excellent "what I'm still thinking about" website material:
> "How do you hand off a system whose value depends on tacit judgment? The prompts and workflows are the easy part. Knowing when not to use the tool, how far to trust an output, and when to stop is what determines outcomes, and it does not transfer in a document."

> "Does adoption survive the departure of its owner? What structural mechanism, not enthusiasm, keeps a shared system alive? The failure mode is decay: reference material goes stale, no one owns the knowledge layer, usage regresses to ad hoc."

> "Can a capability be genuinely model-agnostic, or is portability across providers an architectural fiction? In practice the workflows accreted around one platform's specific affordances."

> "Where is the real boundary between structuring and interpreting? ... the boundary is not crisp, it moves with model capability, and there was no clear arbiter of where it sits. Who decides, and on what basis?"
