## 2. VOICE PROFILE

**The short version:** Drake writes like a bright, funny, slightly over-caffeinated person thinking out loud and then catching himself. He opens with a scene or a flat provocation, spirals through a self-aware digression, and lands a short declarative sentence that reframes the whole thing. He is at his best when he stops performing an essay and just talks.

### Sentence and rhythm

- **Long, then short.** His signature move is three or four winding clauses followed by a 4–8 word verdict. "*It is not about getting attached to your cleverest idea. It is about staying close to the problem until the right solution becomes obvious.*" He does this at the end of nearly every paragraph he cares about.
- **Average sentence ~24 words in essays, ~35 in journals.** The journals run on happily; the essays are pruned.
- **He loves the "not X, but Y" construction** and overuses it: "*The best product is not the one with the most features. It is the one with the clearest value.*" / "*This is not a broken system; it is a deliberately constructed one.*" / "*Clarity is not just a pitching skill. It is a business requirement.*" It's genuinely his, but three per page is too many.
- **Rhetorical questions in clusters.** He asks two or three in a row and then answers one. "*What if we could rewrite the past?  How would our decisions change?  What if our choices are less free than we believe?*"
- **Double spaces after periods**, inconsistently. A real fingerprint of his drafting.

### Humor

Dry, self-deprecating, delivered as an aside — usually in parentheses, a dash, or a sentence tacked on after the serious point has landed. He is funniest when he's being honest about his own failures.

- "*Procrastination. Panick. Better luck next time.*" (his own six-word story about the worst part of writing — the misspelling of "Panic" is, frankly, perfect)
- "*Thursday we also played kickball and got wrecked by Team Sports but we had fun and will get them next time.*"
- "*you can't afford an assistant with your ramen noodles budget*"
- "*I guess building something meaningful is a lot harder than just having a cool product and a good pitch on Shark Tank.*"
- "*P.S. If time travel exists when you're reading this, when you end up going back in time, invest in Bitcoin and don't kill grandpa.*"

He also does a specific thing: **he narrates a hypothetical at length and gets carried away with it.** The Rauschenberg passage is the clearest case — he imagines the artist showing up drunk hoping nobody's home, then imagines the *alternate blank canvases* that would have resulted. That's a fiction writer's instinct hiding in an art history assignment.

### Vocabulary

Plain, concrete, American-college. He reaches for a fancy word maybe twice a piece and it usually reads as reaching ("dearth," "prophesize," "monopolized," "lackadaisical"). His strongest nouns are the ordinary ones: naps, coats, rent, fonts, ramen, kickball, grandma. **Keep him in the register of the concrete noun.** When he goes abstract he goes generic ("intentional choices," "meaningful direction," "long-term growth") and the writing dies.

Signature vocabulary: *routine, present, gravity, ripple effect, clarity, scattered, grind, the process, Future Drake / FD, blend in / stand out, momentum.*

### How he opens

Four modes, all of which work:

1. **Flat provocation.** "*I hate the past.*" / "*Education is not about learning to think for yourself. It's about learning to conform.*"
2. **Cinematic cold open.** "*A wealthy fast fashion CEO sits comfortably in his second yacht, the sun shining, and he orders his butler to fetch him a cocktail.*"
3. **Overheard line.** "*'I only have one rule that I ask you to follow in my classroom: Know when to blend in and know when to stand out.'*"
4. **Genre-refusal.** "*Entrepreneurship gets talked about like it starts with some genius idea, and all it takes is the perfect pitch to have a business. That is not what this semester felt like.*"

### How he closes

Consistently his strongest paragraph. He closes with either **a reversal** or **a direct address**.

- Reversal: "*The monster was never hiding in your closet. It was building it.*"
- Reversal: "*They are what separate a great leader from a brilliant dictator.*"
- Direct address / call: "*We can change the world. We just need to help people care again.*"
- Circling back to the opening image: "*...knowing FD is already there waiting for me.*"

**Tic to know about:** he ends formal pieces with a bare line reading "**Thank You**". It appears on the college essay, both Ascend reflections, and the Jobs write-up. It's a school-submission habit. Cut it on the website — but it tells you he thinks of writing as something handed to a person.

### What he does well

1. **Metaphors built from his own life, not from the metaphor shelf.** The teeter-totter of past/present/future. Getting near the summit and hit by an avalanche. Habits as personal time machines. Memories as movies. These land because he actually thinks in them.
2. **Self-implication.** He puts himself in the dock before he prosecutes anyone else. In the fast fashion essay he admits he bought coats in bulk from China to resell without checking who made them. That paragraph is why the essay works.
3. **He can hear a sentence.** "*Word becomes argument. Beliefs made clear.*"
4. **Honest reporting on his own failure**, without either wallowing or spinning it. The all-nighter passage in the wellness journal is a model of it.

### What he overdoes

1. **"Not X, it's Y" parallelism.** Cut roughly half.
2. **Hedges that soften a good sentence.** "*I think*," "*I guess*," "*probably*," "*sort of*," "*basically*," "*at the end of the day*," "*in a way*." He almost always earns the claim and then apologizes for it. Deleting the hedge usually improves the line. (Exception: "I guess" as a comic beat — that one stays.)
3. **Abstract-noun uplift in final paragraphs.** When he doesn't have a reversal ready, he defaults to "growth / journey / process / best version of myself." That's the failure mode.
4. **Restating the thesis a third time.** Both Ascend drafts say "fall in love with the problem" and then explain it twice more.
5. **Sweeping first-person-plural claims.** "*Everyone knows we are the happiest when we're living in the present*" — he flags this habit himself in WRIT 100 and is actively fixing it.

### Roughness that is a feature — do not edit out

- The misspellings in punchlines ("Panick," "hoping your not home," "calender"). In a *published* piece, fix silently in prose but consider keeping "Panick" in the six-word story, because it's the joke.
- Fragment sentences: "*The perfect crime;*" "*The only solution?  More.*"
- Sudden second person. He slips from "I" to "you" mid-paragraph when he gets urgent. It's not sloppiness; it's how he pulls the reader in.
- The double space after periods, in anything hand-set to look like a manuscript.
- Third-person self-reference for comic distance: "*recruiting Drake*," "*Future Drake*," "*Present Drake*."

## 3. RECURRING THEMES

### A. Future Drake — living for a self who doesn't exist yet
The load-bearing idea of his whole body of work, and it recurs across three years and four documents.

> "My hero is FD.  It's hard to not think about the future, especially when you're not where you want to be in the present.  I always prophesize and think about the future, but I find comfort in my empty promises to FD."
> — *Ordinary Time Traveler*

> "Every decision we make has a ripple effect, having cascading impacts on what we become, why we care, and the overall lives we lead someday."
> — *PHIL 105 Final Letter*

> "when you choose to stay up late binge-watching Dexter instead of preparing for an exam, you're basically traveling forward in time to a future where you're sleep-deprived and unprepared."
> — *PHIL 105 Final Letter*

### B. Presence vs. productivity — the theme he is currently living
This is the one the website should be built around. It runs from a high school teacher's five-minute ritual (2024) to an explicit self-indictment (2025) to an attempted correction (2026).

> "Rarely were these quick jots anything to do with the quote but just what needed to be put to paper and no longer just an idea.  A couple people would share, we'd snap our appreciations, and we'd move on to the purpose of the class."
> — *Ordinary Time Traveler*

> "Now, I still might not be able to regurgitate what we intended to learn in that class, but I do remember appreciating the time to not rush to check all the boxes every day."
> — *Ordinary Time Traveler*

> "This goal matters to me because I get so caught up in being 'productive,' often juggling several things at once and not allowing myself to be present."
> — *Health and Fitness Fundamentals Journal*, week of 10/13

> "Meditation, or simply allowing more silence, is an excellent tool for reducing stress and regaining focus. I fill nearly every minute with noise or stimulation"
> — *PE-125 Final Project*

### C. Energy as the real currency — and where it actually comes from
He has noticed, repeatedly and in writing, that his best days come from movement, people, and laughter — not from output.

> — *Personal Bill of Rights* (sensitive doc — theme only, do not quote publicly without approval)

### D. Blend in / stand out — knowing which mode the moment calls for
The oldest idea he has, from a middle-school Spanish teacher, and it still governs how he describes group work, pitching, and golf.

> "Blending in doesn't mean not being seen or heard, but assimilating and succeeding in any environment. On the other hand, standing out is distinguishing yourself by sharing your creativity and innovation. The rule isn't about having the ability to do both but knowing when to use each one."
> — *Blending in and Standing Out*

### E. Perfectionism as both engine and brake
He names it in four separate documents and treats it as his central flaw.

> "But being a perfectionist makes that tough, too. When I look back, I end up obsessing over the mistakes I made or the decisions I didn't make, and it's probably where most of my stress comes from."
> — *Ordinary Time Traveler*

> "I am a perfectionist, so I critique each word and the overall flow as I'm writing and struggle to let imperfections stay before trying to rework my writing as I go, making it a very slow process but overall being 'better' first drafts."
> — *Writing 100 Prewrites*, 1/29

> "Perfection is okay to strive for but an unhealthy standard to achieve so it's okay to have lofty goals but not goals that one can't ever obtain (small victories)"
> — *Johnson Scholarship Essay* (outline)

### F. Who is actually responsible — systems vs. individuals
His argumentative instinct is always to look past the person to the structure that built the incentive, and then to refuse to let the individual entirely off the hook.

> "Am I so wrong for prioritizing my economic needs over those I have no knowledge I'm affecting? Regardless of ignorance, it is still a problem that can and needs to be addressed."
> — *Fast Fashion Personal Essay*

> "Without reform at the production level, putting all the responsibility onto consumers is not only unrealistic, but it also shields corporations from accountability."
> — *The Monster Was Never In Your Closet*

### G. Clarity as an ethic, not a skill
Appears in writing, pitching, and product work identically.

> "Clarity is not just a pitching skill. It is a business requirement. If your product takes too long to explain, there is a good chance the concept itself is still unclear."
> — *Ascend Final Reflection, Draft #2*

> "I found speed not just in typing, but in decision-making. I became faster because I became clearer and gave myself more time to think."
> — *WRIT 100 Final Reflection*

### H. Family, quietly
Never the subject, always in the frame: his grandma's hugs, watching *Back to the Future* with his dad, pickleball with his mom, pickleball with his dad, his stepdad's W&L stories, dinner with his dad on a Thursday he skipped a workout for. He writes about family in **one clause at a time**, and it works.

## 4. CONTRADICTIONS / TENSIONS

These are the most interesting thing in the corpus. Several are unresolved *in the same document*.

**1. He built his identity on Future Drake and has started to notice the cost.**
*Ordinary Time Traveler* (Oct 2024) presents FD warmly: "*I know it all worked out, and it's going to be okay.*" But the same essay contains the confession "*at the core, I care more about my future self than my present self*" — and the essay's own opening argues that presence is where happiness lives. He states the thesis and undercuts it within four paragraphs and never resolves it. **That unresolved gap is the essay's best feature and the seed of everything he's thinking now.**

**2. "Living in the present is what we're all told" vs. he doesn't do it.**
He can articulate the case for presence beautifully and then, in the wellness journals a year later, describes watching a lecture video while eating lunch. He knows. He says so. He does it anyway. That honesty is publishable.

**3. More system vs. less system.**
*PE-125 Final Project* (Dec 2025) is 2,300 words of Atomic Habits scaffolding — habit stacking, implementation intentions, Notion trackers, whiteboards, a supplement schedule. Seven months later he writes, of himself, that one of his greatest risks is "*excessive optimization*" and "*Plan elaborate systems instead of completing the next concrete step.*" **He built the machine and then diagnosed the machine.** That is the arc.

**4. "I need to be too busy" vs. "I need to protect my attention."**
Sept 2025: "*I need to be too busy so I don't have time to think about what I could be doing better - more action, less planning and judgment.*" July 2026: he writes rules against exactly that. One year, two opposite prescriptions, both sincerely argued.

**5. He hates the past and is constantly writing about it.**
"*I hate the past... if you gave me the chance to go back to any moment, even with all the knowledge I have now, I wouldn't do it.*" Then he spends the next three paragraphs on naps, his grandmother, Santa Claus, and the tooth fairy. He is a nostalgist who has decided not to be.

**6. Consumer complicity vs. producer power.**
Feb 2025 (*Personal Essay*): "*It is our responsibility to know where our clothes come from.*" April 2025 (*The Monster*): "*putting all the responsibility onto consumers is not only unrealistic, but it also shields corporations from accountability.*" He changed his mind inside a single semester, on the record, and can name why. That's an essay in itself.

**7. He hedges hardest on the claims he believes most.**
The Bitcoin postscript, the Shark Tank line, "I guess you could say with great power... comes great responsibility" — he reaches for a joke exactly when the point is sincerest. Worth knowing when editing: the hedge is protective, not decorative.

**8. Two registers, one person.**
Compare *Wellness Journal* ("*got wrecked by Team Sports but we had fun*") with the Mountaingate LinkedIn draft ("*I'm most energized when I can combine analytical thinking with building*"). The second is not him. The professional voice he is being handed is flatter than the voice he already has. **The website's job is to make the first voice the professional one.**

## 5. NOTES FOR WHOEVER WRITES THE SITE COPY

- Short paragraphs. He writes in 4–7 sentence blocks and the rhythm depends on it.
- Let a fragment land. Don't smooth "The only solution?  More." into a full sentence.
- Concrete nouns over category nouns. "Naps," not "rest habits." "Coats from China," not "consumption choices."
