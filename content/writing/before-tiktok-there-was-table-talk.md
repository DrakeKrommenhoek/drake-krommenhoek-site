*Shuffling cards, playing tunes, laughter echoing through the room.*
*Sister mad, mother sad, dad and I choose the movie, sealing their doom.*

Growing up in a game-playing family, competition wasn't optional; it was genetic. From poker nights to euchre fights, there was never a dull night in the Krommenhoek household. The only TikTok we knew was the timer ticking down as you tried to draw your best elephant riding a skateboard in Pictionary.

While most teens were reading comments, we were reading each other's faces, trying to decode a bluff or hide our own. There's nothing more memorable than watching my sister bite her tongue, trying not to smile, and giving away her hand. Win or lose, there was never a bad night at the card table, unless your uncle flipped it after losing to quads with a full house (he still claims it was an accident; I disagree).

Those game nights weren't just fun; they were the foundations of what makes my family so close today. They built patience, taught me to read people in conversations, and turned my risk-taking into a basic instinct. Every shuffle, an understanding of the unpredictability that comes with each and every day. Every hand dealt, a reminder that you don't have to take them on alone. Connection used to mean eye contact, laughter, and catching up on each other's weeks. Now it's measured by how close you are to the Wi-Fi router.

Somewhere between the shuffle of cards and the scroll of feeds, we traded real presence for a digital popularity contest. Our generation didn't mean or want to abandon our family or friends; we just followed where the noise was the loudest. And honestly, who can blame us? It's easier to scroll through noise than sit with silence. Likes quickly replaced laughter, notifications bring us a smile, and our self-worth comes from those who double-tap rather than those who double-check how we're actually doing.

## The Lost Art of Family Game Night

When did families stop prioritizing table talk and laughter and start calling Prime Day the only "deal" worth celebrating?

These days, family nights look more like five people in one room, each in a different world. Dad's watching sports, Mom's scouring Amazon, my sister's making sure she doesn't lose her snap streaks, and I'm constantly "leveling up" on my phone. I'm unsure of what's actually being played, the game, or myself.

We've built a culture where proximity trumps being present, and attention is the rarest and most valuable currency we possess.

According to Exploding Topics, the average person now spends 6 hours 40 minutes per day on their devices. As Americans, we top that at 7 hours 3 minutes. But Gen Z? We don't like to lose at anything, and clock in at around 9 hours daily. Nearly half our waking lives are spent looking at a screen, often looking for the next hit of temporary dopamine.

And it's not just the quantity of time; it's what it's replacing. A recent national study by Talker Research found that American households are "overconnected but under-engaged," showing that devices once meant to bring families together are now isolating them in their own personalized digital bubbles. Parents report feeling "constantly distracted," kids feel ignored, and quality family time is quietly being replaced by periods of withdrawal from scrolling.

The trade-off is subtle but dangerous. We haven't stopped craving connection; we've just reinvented it as text messages and phone calls, when we should be prioritizing "Face time."

## The Loneliness Epidemic

Poker taught me risk long before Econ 100 did. Euchre taught me to read and rely on a partner, bluffing taught me emotional control, and losing taught me humility. Lessons no algorithm can replicate. Every night around that table felt like a class in basic life skills, spent with those I love most.

There's a strange irony to realizing that Gen Z, the generation that can decode messages from thousands of abbreviations and emojis, can't read a room when a family member isn't acting like themselves.

According to recent data from GDI, 80 percent of Gen Z say they've felt lonely in the past year, even though we're the most digitally "connected" generation in history. Our phones give us constant access to our best friends to our grandma, but we've sacrificed intimacy in exchange for convenience.

We can text "LOL" all day without ever really laughing.

It's easier to react than reach out. Easier to say you're fine than ask for help.

## Shrinking Attention Spans

When you grow up on screens, attention becomes a luxury to still have after years of battling short-form media. A study from Golden Steps ABA claims the average attention span has fallen to about 8 seconds, shorter than a goldfish's. Sure, that makes for a catchy headline, but it also sheds light on a deeper issue: we've trained ourselves to crave constant stimulation and to avoid sustained presence that doesn't hit our dopamine button.

Family game nights were the opposite. You had to stay in it, read the room, take a few punches, and wait for your turn to strike. There was no skip button when the tension began to build.

And maybe that's why those nights mattered so much. They weren't about winning as much as they were about surviving. Paying attention. Practicing connection in real time.

## Reclaiming Our Focus

Maybe the solution isn't deleting TikTok or hiding your phone in a drawer. Maybe it's shuffling a deck instead.

Every Friday night, my family still pulls out a deck of cards, or if we really want to mix it up, we'll dust off our copy of Catan. It's not about nostalgia; it's about reclaiming our focus and prioritizing connection. In Catan, you learn risk analysis, trade, and strategy in real time, not from a YouTube tutorial, but from understanding and reacting to constantly changing circumstances. You learn how to weigh opportunity costs, trade resources, and that even one brick or wheat can be the difference between winning and losing.

If you only have two players, swap the resources for a chessboard. Chess demands patience and long-term planning. There are no shortcuts, no swiping, no "for you" page to tell you what move to make. It's a constant battle between ego (taking pieces early with no thought to late-game positioning) and delayed gratification: making calculated, smart plays in hopes of a late-game victory.

Poker, on the other hand, teaches humility and hope. There's a saying my mom would always use (after her third re-buy):

> All you need is a chip and a chair.

You can be nearly out of the game and still win if you're patient, strategic, and just lucky enough to stay alive. It's the perfect metaphor for modern resilience: knowing when to go all in, when to fold, and how to keep your composure no matter what cards you're dealt.

A 2023 study in the *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* found that card and board games significantly improve executive functions like working memory, inhibition control, and cognitive flexibility, the same mental muscles that help us focus and manage emotions in daily life. Similarly, a literature review in the *Simulation & Gaming Journal* found that games like Catan and chess foster strategic planning, problem-solving, and adaptive reasoning, strengthening communication skills, patience, and teamwork.

Maybe that's why playing cards feels like therapy to me. It pulls me out of my screen and back into shared space surrounded by those I love most. It's a guaranteed hour where I focus on them, not on the algorithm working 24/7 to steal my attention.

So before you try another digital or dopamine detox, maybe just shuffle up and deal yourself a better hand with those who matter most.
