Golf is the cruelest of games because it removes every excuse.

There is no defense to blame, no bad pass to point to, no teammate who didn't rotate on time. There is only you and the ball and the distance between where it is and where you wanted it to go. The game is a mirror, and mirrors are uncomfortable.

I have been playing since I was eight. I have loved it and hated it and walked away from it and come back to it. And somewhere in those years, I started to understand something about pressure that I have never been able to learn from a book.

## The Scoreboard Doesn't Lie

In golf, as in business, there is a scoreboard. It is public. It is updated in real time. And it does not care about your intentions.

You can have the most technically sound swing on the range and shoot 82 on the course. You can have the most compelling pitch deck and still not close the round. The distance between preparation and performance is where everything interesting happens — and it is a distance that only gets crossed under conditions of actual consequence.

I have watched intelligent, capable people freeze under pressure and wonder why. I think the answer is usually this: they have trained for the conditions of the range, not the conditions of the course. They have practiced when the outcome didn't matter and found themselves undone when it did.

## The Routine as Anchor

The best players I have watched — not on television, but up close, in competition — share something. They have a pre-shot routine that does not vary. The routine is not superstition. It is infrastructure.

When the putt matters, the mind wants to fill the silence with noise. With doubt, with calculation, with the memory of the last time you missed from this distance. The routine is how you shut that noise out. Not by suppressing it, but by filling the space with something procedural, something practiced, something that does not require the prefrontal cortex to be operating at full capacity.

I think about this when I walk into high-stakes situations that aren't on a golf course. A presentation to investors. A difficult conversation with a partner. A decision that needs to be made under uncertainty.

What is my routine? What is the anchor that keeps me from drowning in the noise?

## Comfort With Discomfort

The thing that separates good competitors from the rest is not that they feel less pressure. It is that they have learned to perform inside of it.

There is no shortcut to this. You learn it by putting yourself in uncomfortable situations repeatedly, by failing in front of people, by taking the second shot when you'd rather pick up the ball and walk off the green.

Golf taught me this. So did entrepreneurship. So did speaking in front of a room when I wasn't sure I had anything worth saying.

The pressure doesn't go away. You just get bigger than it.
