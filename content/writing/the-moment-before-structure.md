There is a moment — brief, almost invisible — that comes before a thing becomes what it is.

Before a company has its culture. Before a team has its rhythm. Before a relationship has its language. There is a window, usually a few weeks or months, when everything is still soft. When the norms haven't calcified, when the habits haven't formed, when the shape of the thing is still being decided by every small choice made in every unremarkable moment.

I have been thinking about this a lot lately.

## The Invisible Founding

Most founders obsess over product. Some obsess over hiring. Fewer obsess over culture — not the culture deck, not the values printed on the wall, but the actual lived texture of how people treat one another, how disagreement sounds, how failure is absorbed, how credit moves around a room.

And yet it is that texture, more than any slide deck or strategic framework, that determines what a company becomes.

The first time someone takes credit for another's work and no one says anything — that is a founding moment. The first time a hard conversation is avoided in favor of comfort — that is a founding moment. The first time someone is rewarded for being loud rather than being right — that is a founding moment.

None of these feel significant at the time. They rarely do.

## Honor as Infrastructure

At Washington and Lee, honor isn't an aspiration. It is infrastructure. You do not lock your laptop in the library. You do not second-guess whether the test in your bag is truly unseen. You simply operate in a world where the assumption of integrity is baked into the architecture of everyday life.

I didn't appreciate what this was until I left it briefly — until I encountered contexts where trust had to be earned differently, where the default assumption wasn't goodness but caution.

The contrast was illuminating. Honor, it turns out, is not just a moral category. It is an operational one. When people trust one another at the base level, the friction of ordinary collaboration drops significantly. You can move faster. You can be more honest. You can take more risk, because the floor beneath you is solid.

The question I carry into every organization I join or build: *What is the default assumption here? What does the architecture of this place say about what is expected?*

## The Moment Before Structure

Here is what I have come to believe: you have far more influence over culture in the first thirty days than in the next three years.

Not because people are more impressionable early on — though they are — but because the structures haven't formed yet. The habits, the shortcuts, the unspoken agreements about what is acceptable and what isn't. All of that is still being written.

Every choice you make in that window is a small act of authorship.

Do you say the hard thing in the meeting, or do you save it for the parking lot afterward? Do you celebrate the person who admits the mistake, or do you quietly hold it against them? Do you let the loudest voice win, or do you create conditions for the quietest voice to be heard?

You might not feel like you're doing anything important. You might feel like you're just getting through Tuesday.

But Tuesday, in the early days of a thing, is when it is decided.

## What This Means Practically

I am not sure I have a clean prescription here. What I have are a few questions I try to ask myself when I am in that early window of a new thing:

**What behavior am I rewarding?** Not with formal recognition — with my attention, my laughter, my follow-up questions. We reward what we notice.

**What am I tolerating?** The first time something uncomfortable happens and passes without comment, it becomes a precedent. The second time, it becomes a norm.

**Who am I putting at the center?** Every early team develops a center of gravity — the person whose opinion is sought first, whose comfort matters most. That person becomes, whether intentionally or not, the north star of the culture. Choose carefully.

---

None of this is particularly original. The idea that culture is set early, that honor is operational, that founding moments are often invisible — smarter people than me have written about all of this.

What I find is that knowing it and living it are different things. That the moment before structure takes hold tends to pass quietly, without announcement. And that the people who shape the best organizations are the ones who noticed the window was open.
